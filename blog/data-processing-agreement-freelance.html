Do You Need a DPA? Data Processing for Freelance AI Work, Explained

Published 2026-08-03 · 4 min read · legal contracts

You just landed a retainer building a RAG chatbot over a client's support tickets. Halfway through onboarding, their ops lead emails: "Can you send over your DPA?" If your reaction is a quiet panic-Google, this guide is for you.

What a DPA actually is

A Data Processing Agreement is a contract that kicks in whenever you handle personal data on behalf of someone else. Under GDPR the client is the controller (they decide why the data is processed) and you are the processor (you do the work with it). Article 28 says that relationship must be governed by a written contract. That contract is the DPA.

It is not a privacy policy, and it is not the same thing as an NDA. An NDA says "don't blab." A DPA says "here is exactly how you may touch this data, where it may live, who else may see it, and what happens when the engagement ends."

Do you need one? A quick test

Ask yourself these in order:

  • Does the work involve data that can identify a living person — names, emails, support transcripts, CRM records, call recordings, HR files, user IDs tied to behaviour?
  • Does that data belong to the client (or their users), not to you?
  • Do you touch it at all — store it, pipe it through an API, fine-tune on it, or even just look at it while debugging?

Three yeses means you need a DPA. Note the third one: "I only read the CSV to write the schema" is still processing. So is passing a prompt containing customer names to OpenAI.

You can genuinely skip it when you work exclusively with synthetic or fully anonymised data, or when the client hands you nothing but their own business documents with no personal data inside. Pseudonymised is not anonymised — if a key exists somewhere that can re-link the records, GDPR still applies.

The sub-processor trap

This is where AI consultants get caught. Every model API, vector DB, and hosting provider you route client data through is a sub-processor, and you need the controller's permission to use them. A typical stack quietly involves four or five:

  • The model provider (OpenAI, Anthropic, Azure OpenAI, Bedrock)
  • The vector store (Pinecone, Weaviate, pgvector on some cloud)
  • Your hosting / compute provider
  • Observability and logging tools — LangSmith, Sentry, Datadog
  • Anything that emails or Slacks output containing personal data

Logging tools are the most commonly forgotten. If your traces capture full prompts, your observability vendor is processing personal data. List them all in an annex to the DPA and commit to notifying the client before you add another.

What belongs in the document

A workable freelance DPA is three to five pages, not thirty. Cover:

  • Scope annex — categories of data subjects, categories of data, purpose, and duration. Be specific: "support ticket text and requester email addresses, for the purpose of building and evaluating a retrieval system, for the term of the SOW."
  • Instructions — you process only on documented instruction from the controller.
  • Security measures — encryption in transit and at rest, access control, MFA, disk encryption on your laptop. Say what you actually do; don't copy an enterprise list you can't honour.
  • Sub-processors — the list above, plus a notice period for changes.
  • Breach notification — you tell them "without undue delay," commonly framed as within 24 or 48 hours of becoming aware.
  • Transfers — if data leaves the EEA/UK, name the mechanism (Standard Contractual Clauses, or the provider's own DPA covering it).
  • Deletion and return — what happens at the end, with a deadline.
  • Audit and assistance — cap this. "Reasonable cooperation, one audit per year, at controller's cost" protects a solo operator from an open-ended obligation.

Two clauses worth negotiating hard

First, liability. Many client DPAs carve data breaches out of the SOW's liability cap entirely, leaving you personally exposed to unlimited damages. Push to have DPA liability sit inside the master agreement's cap.

Second, training rights. Make it explicit that you do not train models on their data, and that your sub-processors are configured not to either. For most APIs that means using the enterprise or zero-retention tier, not the consumer default.

Make it operationally true

A signed DPA you violate on day one is worse than none. The commonest gap is logging personal data by accident. Scrub before it leaves your process:

PII = re.compile(r"[\w.+-]+@[\w-]+\.[\w.]+|\+?\d[\d\s-]{8,}\d") def safe_log(prompt): logger.info(PII.sub("[redacted]", prompt)[:2000])

Pair that with retention limits on your vector store, a client-specific folder that gets deleted at project close, and disk encryption you can actually confirm is on.

Getting one signed without a lawyer bill

Most mid-sized clients will send you their DPA. Reading and redlining theirs is usually faster and cheaper than pushing your own. Read every clause on liability, audit rights, and deletion deadlines — those are the three that cost freelancers money. For smaller clients who have nothing, bring your own short template; it makes you look like the most professional vendor on their shortlist.

Bill the review time. Contract review is billable work, and clients who ask for a DPA fully expect a serious vendor to spend an hour on it.
