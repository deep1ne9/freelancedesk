Why Your AI Consulting Contract Needs a Liability Cap (and What to Set)

Published 2026-08-10 · 4 min read · legal contracts

You built a retrieval pipeline for a client. Six months later their support bot quotes a wrong refund policy to 4,000 customers, and someone in their legal department starts a sentence with "our damages are..." If your contract has no limitation of liability clause, the end of that sentence is everything you own. A liability cap is the single highest-leverage paragraph in an AI consulting agreement, and most freelancers still ship contracts without one.

What a liability cap actually does

A limitation of liability clause sets a ceiling on what a client can recover from you if the engagement goes wrong. It does not make you immune, and it does not excuse fraud or gross negligence — no court will enforce that. What it does is convert an unbounded, uninsurable business risk into a known, priced number.

Two separate mechanisms do the work, and you need both:

  • The cap — a hard dollar ceiling on total damages.
  • The consequential damages waiver — excludes lost profits, lost revenue, lost data, and business interruption entirely, regardless of the cap.

The waiver matters more than people expect. In AI work, direct damages (your fees) are small; the scary numbers are always downstream — a mispriced quote engine, a hallucinated compliance answer, a churned enterprise account. Without the waiver, a $20k project can generate a $2M claim.

What number should you set?

There is a market standard, and it is narrower than you think:

  • Fees paid — the total actually paid to you under the agreement. Cleanest and most defensible for small engagements.
  • Fees paid in the preceding 12 months — the norm for retainers and ongoing MLOps work, so year-three liability doesn't compound.
  • A multiple (1x–2x fees) — what you concede when a mid-market client pushes back. Rarely worth fighting past 2x.
  • A flat figure — e.g. $50,000, useful when fees are tiny but the client wants a real number to point at.

My default for solo AI consulting: cap at total fees paid, 12-month lookback, with a full consequential damages waiver, mutual. Make it mutual. Symmetric clauses get signed with far less negotiation than one-sided ones, and you rarely have a large claim against a client anyway.

Sample language

LIMITATION OF LIABILITY. Except for the Excluded Claims, (a) neither
party shall be liable for any indirect, incidental, special,
consequential, or punitive damages, or for lost profits, revenue,
or data, however caused; and (b) each party's total aggregate
liability arising out of or related to this Agreement shall not
exceed the total fees paid by Client to Consultant under this
Agreement in the twelve (12) months preceding the event giving
rise to the claim.

Have a lawyer in your jurisdiction review before you rely on it. This is a starting point, not legal advice.

The carve-outs clients will ask for

Expect the client's counsel to pull certain claims outside the cap. Some are reasonable; some will quietly destroy the protection you just negotiated.

  • Accept: fraud, willful misconduct, gross negligence, bodily injury, and a party's payment obligations. These are unenforceable to cap anyway.
  • Negotiate hard: IP infringement indemnity. Uncapped IP indemnity is dangerous in AI work — you often can't prove what a foundation model was trained on. Push for it to be capped at a multiple of fees, or explicitly scope it to code you personally authored, excluding model outputs and third-party OSS.
  • Resist: "breach of confidentiality" and "breach of data protection obligations" carved out uncapped. These sound narrow but cover the most likely real incident in an AI engagement. Counter with a super-cap: capped at 2x–3x fees rather than unlimited.

AI-specific clauses that make the cap hold

A cap protects you at the back end. These clauses reduce the odds you ever reach it:

  • Model output disclaimer — state plainly that LLM outputs are probabilistic, may be inaccurate, and are not warranted for accuracy or fitness.
  • Human-in-the-loop requirement — make client review of AI output in regulated, financial, medical, or legal contexts a client obligation, not a nice-to-have.
  • Third-party dependency exclusion — you are not liable for OpenAI/Anthropic/AWS outages, price changes, deprecations, or ToS changes.
  • Training data representations flow from the client — if they hand you a dataset, they warrant they have the rights to it.

Practical checklist before you sign

  1. Is there a cap at all, and is it mutual?
  2. Is there a separate consequential damages waiver?
  3. Are carve-outs limited to fraud, willful misconduct, and payment?
  4. Is IP indemnity capped or scoped away from model outputs?
  5. Does the cap survive termination? (It should — add it to the survival clause.)
  6. Does your E&O / professional liability insurance limit exceed the cap you agreed to?

That last one is the tell of a professional. If you cap at $250k but carry $100k of coverage, you have created a $150k personal exposure with your own signature. Align the two, or lower the cap.

The negotiation move that works

When a client pushes for an uncapped agreement, don't argue about fairness — reprice. "I can work uncapped, but I'd need to carry substantially more coverage and my rate goes from $180 to $310/hour to fund it. Most clients prefer the capped version." Nine times out of ten they take the cap. You've reframed liability as what it actually is: a line item, not a moral position.
